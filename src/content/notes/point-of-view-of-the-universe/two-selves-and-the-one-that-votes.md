Daniel Kahneman noticed something most people, including most psychologists, had overlooked. When you ask someone how an experience was, you are not asking the person who underwent it. You are asking a different person entirely — one who arrived afterward, looked at what the experiencing person had been through, and constructed a summary to file away for future reference.

Kahneman called these the *experiencing self* and the *remembering self*. The experiencing self is whoever is having the experience right now, moment to moment. The remembering self is whoever shows up later to evaluate it, narrate it, and use it as input to decisions. They are different agents in the same head, operating at different timescales, by different rules, and very often arriving at different verdicts.

The cleanest demonstration comes from a study on colonoscopies, conducted back when the procedure was significantly more painful than it is now. Two groups of patients underwent the same procedure, with one difference: one group's procedure ended at the usual point, while the other group's procedure had a few extra minutes of mild discomfort tacked on at the end. The second group, objectively, underwent more pain. They had every second of the first group's experience *plus* additional minutes of suffering.

When asked afterward how bad the procedure had been, the second group reported it as *less* bad. They also expressed more willingness to undergo a repeat. This is the peak-end rule in action: the remembering self averages peak intensity with how the experience ended, and the second group's ending was milder.

The experiencing self of the second group suffered more. The remembering self of the second group reported less suffering. And — this is the part that should bother you — the remembering self is the one that gets to decide whether to come back for another procedure.

This is not a minor processing quirk. It's structural. The experiencing self has no mechanism by which to influence future decisions. By the time decisions are being made, the experiencing self is already in the past, and the remembering self has assumed full control of the narrative. The agent that *underwent* the suffering does not get to vote on whether suffering of that kind is acceptable in the future. The agent that *constructed* a summary of it does.

Once you see this, you start seeing it everywhere. The vacation remembered as wonderful that contained long stretches of boredom and argument. The job remembered as fulfilling that involved years of low-grade dread. The relationship remembered as good because of how it ended, even though most of its days were difficult. The pattern is consistent: peaks and endings remembered, durations and middles forgotten, and the forgetting is what drives the next decision.

This raises a question that hedonist welfare theory owes us an answer to, and has not really paid: *which self's testimony counts?*

If wellbeing is constituted by felt experience, the natural answer is the experiencing self. The experiencing self is the one actually doing the feeling. The remembering self is, at best, a flawed reporter on what the experiencing self underwent. Privileging the remembering self's verdict over the experiencing self's reality is privileging a summary over the thing being summarised. For a hedonist, that should look like a category error.

But there's a serious counter-move. The remembering self is the one with continuity, the one with projects and plans, the one who actually constructs a life out of moments. The experiencing self, if you slow down to look at it, fragments. There is no continuous moment-to-moment experiencer; there is just a stream of present experiences with no narrative thread connecting them. Maybe what we call "a life going well" is irreducibly a remembering-self construct. Maybe the experiencing self, considered in isolation, isn't the kind of thing that *can* have a life going well or badly. Maybe wellbeing requires the narrative scaffolding that only the remembering self provides.

This is a serious response. It also makes a substantial concession that the strict hedonist has to acknowledge. If wellbeing requires narrative — if a sequence of moments only constitutes a life going well when there's a remembering self stringing them together — then wellbeing isn't just felt experience. It's felt experience *plus* something the remembering self contributes. That's a step away from pure hedonism, in the direction of a hybrid theory.

Singer and de Lazari-Radek lean toward the experiencing self in their treatment but don't push the argument to its conclusion. The honest conclusion is that strict hedonism is in trouble either way. Privilege the experiencing self, and you have to explain why the remembering self gets to make all the decisions. Privilege the remembering self, and you have to explain why summary-construction has more moral weight than the underlying experience.

There's a deeper version of this worry that connects to Parfit. If the self is, as Parfit argues, already a bundle of overlapping psychological continuities rather than a single persisting entity, then "experiencing self vs remembering self" is just one of many ways the bundle decomposes. There is no privileged "real you" doing the experiencing; there is no privileged "real you" doing the remembering. There are streams of psychological process that we narrativise into a person. On this picture, the question "which self gets the vote?" doesn't have a clean answer because the self that would adjudicate doesn't exist as a separate further fact.

That's a destabilising thought, but it's also clarifying. If there's no underlying self to settle the dispute, then welfare theory has to make a substantive choice: weight experiences directly, or weight narratives, or some mix. Pretending we can recover the answer from "what the person really cares about" doesn't work, because the person is exactly what's in dispute. The remembering self cares about narratives; the experiencing self cares about moments; both are real and neither is more authentically you than the other.

For most policy purposes, the experiencing self is probably what should matter. Healthcare decisions, labour conditions, the treatment of animals — these all involve large quantities of moment-to-moment suffering that the remembering self systematically under-counts, including in the people undergoing them. If you build policy around what subjects *report* in retrospect, you'll consistently under-invest in reducing the kinds of suffering whose duration and unspectacularness make them invisible to the remembering self. The experiencing self has no voice in surveys, but it has every second of the experience.

For most personal-life purposes, by contrast, the remembering self is what we live by. We choose our next vacation based on how the last one is remembered, not based on what experience-sampling would have recorded. We decide to stay or leave a job based on the constructed narrative, not the moment-to-moment hedonic data. There's no obvious way to change this, and it's not clear that we should want to. A life entirely run by the experiencing self might be one that never makes any long-term commitments at all.

These don't sit comfortably together. Any serious welfare theory has to say something about the gap. So far, almost none have, and the result is that we tacitly default to remembering-self welfare in nearly every domain that matters. The experiencing self stays silent, as it always has, while the remembering self goes on casting votes on its behalf.
