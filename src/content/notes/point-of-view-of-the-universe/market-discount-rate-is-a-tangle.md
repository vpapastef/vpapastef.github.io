Cost-benefit analysis runs on discount rates. The orthodox justification is that we should use *market* discount rates — anything else, the argument goes, would be paternalistic. We should respect what people actually prefer, and markets are where preferences get revealed.

Nordhaus famously uses a social discount rate around 5.5%. Compounded over a century, that implies one life saved today is worth roughly 239 lives saved in 2125. That's not a rhetorical flourish; that's what the arithmetic delivers.

The whole argument depends on a measurement claim: that market behavior tells us what people's *pure time preference* actually is. It doesn't. What you see in the market is a bundle, and you can't recover the components from the bundle.

When someone in a market accepts a 5% rate, you are seeing at least four different things added together.

**One: uncertainty about being alive next year.** A 70-year-old with a heart condition will demand more compensation for delay than a 25-year-old. Not because their *time preference* is different. Because the 70-year-old might not be around to collect.

**Two: risk that the macroeconomic situation collapses.** Recession, war, currency crisis, government default. A dollar in a working economy is worth more than a dollar in a collapsing one, and the market prices this in.

**Three: expected wealth growth combined with diminishing marginal utility.** If you expect to be richer next year, an extra dollar then is worth less than an extra dollar now — and you need to be compensated to defer.

**Four: genuine pure time preference.** The brute fact that some people just intrinsically prefer the present.

The headline rate bundles all four. There is no way, from observation alone, to know how much of the 5% is which.

Concrete example. You offer me €100 today or €105 in a year. I take the €100. An economist concludes I have a 5% time preference. But my actual reasoning might have been: *I'm 70 and might not be here in a year.* That's probability discounting, not time preference. The observed behavior is identical; the underlying reason is completely different.

For an individual decision, the distinction may not matter. For a policy decision about how to weigh present humans against future ones, it matters enormously — because future *humanity* isn't expected to die in a year. The reasons that legitimately make me discount my own future utility don't transfer to the social planner's problem of weighing present lives against future lives. To treat the market rate as a measure of pure time preference, and then apply that rate to intergenerational policy, is to launder probability-discounting and wealth-expectation into a policy parameter that pretends to be about something else entirely.

So "use the market rate, that's neutral" is doing two illegitimate things at once. It's pretending to measure something it isn't measuring. And it's pretending to be value-free when it isn't.

The second pretense is the bigger one. Suppose, for the sake of argument, we could disentangle the four components and recover a clean pure-time-preference number. Should we use it for social discounting?

Take Maria. When Maria shops, she buys cheap fast fashion. It's available, it's affordable, she likes new things. When Maria votes, she supports politicians who want to regulate fast fashion into oblivion because of its environmental and labour consequences. Maria is not inconsistent. As a consumer, she expresses immediate convenience preferences under limited deliberation and limited information. As a citizen, she expresses what kind of society she wants to live in, what rules she's willing to be bound by collectively, what trade-offs she endorses on reflection.

If a policy analyst wants to know what Maria's preferences are, the answer is: *which Maria?* Consumer Maria and Citizen Maria have different preferences, and the analyst has to choose which one to use. Picking the consumer's revealed time preference is not a neutral methodological move. It's a substantive ethical choice about whose voice — present consumer-self or reflective citizen-self — gets to write the policy.

The "we're just respecting people's preferences" framing hides this choice rather than addressing it. The choice is real either way; the question is whether the analyst acknowledges making it.

Cost-benefit analysis is doing ethics. It is not measuring ethics. The pretence that it's the latter is what allows policies with 5.5% rates to be defended as technical rather than moral. The arithmetic is technical. The choice of what to put into the arithmetic is moral, all the way down.
