In 1903, a philosopher named G.E. Moore noticed something strange about the word "good."

Pick anything you might think "good" really means. Pleasure. Survival. What evolution selected for. Behaviour that helps people get along. Now ask: but is *that* good? The question doesn't sound silly. It's a real question. You can keep asking it, no matter what you fill in.

That's strange, because most words don't behave this way. Take "bachelor." A bachelor is an unmarried man, and once you know that, asking "but is an unmarried man really a bachelor?" sounds confused. The question closes. There's nothing left to ask.

With "good," the question never closes. Moore took this as a clue. Maybe "good" doesn't translate into anything else. Maybe it's its own thing: a real property, but not one you can swap out for "pleasure" or "survival" or any other description from the natural world.

People who think "good" does translate into something natural have pushed back. Maybe *good = pleasure* is a true equation we haven't proved yet, the way "water" turned out to mean H₂O long after the word already existed. Once chemists worked it out, the question "is water really H₂O?" closed. Maybe the same will happen with "good."

But here is what doesn't fit. With water, the question did close once we knew the chemistry. With "good," no proposed equation closes it. Say *good = pleasure* and a few pages later you'll be asking whether a brain hooked up to a machine that produces nothing but artificial pleasure is *actually* good. Say *good = what people prefer* and addiction cases reopen the question. The closure arrives, but it doesn't last.

Maybe that's because we haven't found the right answer yet. Or maybe it's telling us something. Maybe "good" isn't the kind of thing that translates into the natural world at all.

---

But if "good" isn't a natural property, what is it? Something invisible? Something only some people can sense? This is where the comparison with mathematics is useful.

Mathematical truths aren't physical. You can't measure *2 + 2 = 4* with an instrument. You can't find it under a microscope. It's not in the natural world the way water is. And yet most of us think it's real anyway. Two plus two does equal four. Mathematicians aren't making things up; they're discovering something.

How can that be? How is math both real *and* not part of the natural world?

Notice, first, that we already live with the answer. We reach mathematical truths by thinking rather than by looking. No experiment settles whether there are infinitely many primes; you work it out. Once you have, it holds everywhere, including in places nobody will ever visit and for quantities nobody will ever count. None of this makes us suspect the result was invented.

Ethics has the same shape. Gratuitously hurting someone is bad. Suffering matters. Similar cases should be treated alike. You don't establish these by measurement either. You consider them, they hold up, and they keep holding up when you try to think your way around them.

Henry Sidgwick, writing in the 1870s, called moral starting points of this kind self-evident propositions. He didn't mean obvious. He meant claims that survive careful reflection without needing support from anything more basic, which is a demanding test and one most moral intuitions fail.

| Mathematics | Ethics |
| --- | --- |
| There are infinitely many primes | Gratuitous cruelty is bad |
| Worked out by reflection, not measured | Worked out by reflection, not measured |
| Holds for numbers nobody will ever count | Holds for people you will never meet |

A caution about how far to push this. Mathematicians do not treat their starting points as untouchable: deny Euclid's parallel postulate and you get a geometry that turned out to describe actual space. So the parallel isn't that both fields rest on unquestionable foundations. It's narrower, and it survives that point. In both fields we reach the foundations by reflection rather than observation, and in both we treat what we find as discovered rather than invented.

---

A lot of people quietly hold a lopsided position: math is real and objective; ethics is opinion. If both fields reach their foundations the same way, that lopsidedness needs a reason. The usual candidates (math has proofs, math gives us bridges and computers, mathematicians agree) don't break the parallel where it's claimed. They break it higher up, where reasoning has already been built on top of the foundations. The foundations themselves are reached the same way in both.

You could escape this by saying: fine, ethics has the same structure as math, but math doesn't deserve the "real and objective" status you think it does. Math is symbol-shuffling. That works, but it pays for denying ethics's objectivity by giving up math's.

Most working mathematicians don't, in their bones, believe their work is symbol-shuffling. They take their results as discoveries. If they're right, then by the same reasoning ethics gets the same status. Not because we've declared it. Just by being consistent about how we treat foundations.

---

There's a possible explanation for why the lopsided position is common, and it's worth sitting with. Accepting math costs almost nothing personally. The same math that built computers, antibiotics, and GPS also tells us what's happening to the climate. We use the smartphones. We often ignore the climate. The math hasn't become less reliable. The implications have become more inconvenient.

So maybe the asymmetry isn't about which fields give us truth. Maybe it's about which truths are comfortable to live with.

---

That's the case Moore was reaching for, in plainer terms. The view isn't strange. It asks: if you're willing to grant that math gives us real truths even though they're not physical, why aren't you willing to do the same for ethics?

Both are reached by careful thinking, extend by reasoning, and produce results that hold up under scrutiny.

If you find yourself sympathetic to math being real, and most people are even if they don't think about it much, then the question is why you've drawn the line where you have.

And something turns on where you draw it. If ethics works like math, then moral disagreement isn't a clash of preference; some positions are more carefully reasoned than others, and some get closer to the truth. The next time you catch yourself dismissing a moral claim as *"just someone's opinion,"* ask whether you'd say the same of a mathematical claim you didn't yet understand.
