Evolutionary debunking is a powerful tool. It runs like this: if our belief in some moral claim P is produced by an evolutionary process, and the evolutionary process doesn't track the truth of P, then we have reason to be suspicious of our belief in P. The mechanism that produced the belief explains the belief without invoking its truth, which leaves the belief epistemically homeless.

The tool was developed largely by anti-realists — Sharon Street and Richard Joyce most prominently — who wanted to undermine moral realism by showing that our moral intuitions look exactly like what a process that doesn't track moral truth would produce. The point is meant to be defensive: if you can't tell the difference between intuitions tracking real moral facts and intuitions reflecting evolutionary pressures, why bet on the former?

Utilitarians have noticed that the tool can be repointed.

Singer and de Lazari-Radek, in *The Point of View of the Universe*, run debunking arguments aggressively against various common-sense moral intuitions. The intuition that we owe more to our kin than to strangers? Plausibly evolved from inclusive-fitness pressures, doesn't track moral truth, debunked. The intuition that we owe more to compatriots than to foreigners? Tribal-coalition selection, debunked. The intuition that the "virtuous" deserve happiness in a way the "vicious" don't? An evolved disposition to punish defectors and reward cooperators, debunked. The intuition that physical contact (Singer's drowning child) matters more than statistical distance? A perceptual bias evolution had no reason to override, debunked.

The pattern is clean and the conclusions are useful, because every one of these debunked intuitions stood in the way of an impartial utilitarian conclusion. Debunk them, and the utilitarian verdict gets a clearer run.

But notice what the same authors do *not* debunk.

The intuition that pleasure is good. The intuition that suffering is bad. The intuition that more good is better than less good. Sidgwick's axiom of rational benevolence. These are treated as either self-evident, or as so deeply secured by rational reflection that no debunking move could touch them.

This is the selectivity problem. The exact same tool that demolishes the intuitions opposing utilitarianism is held back from the intuitions supporting it. The asymmetry isn't argued for in any deep way. It's mostly *stipulated* — these intuitions are "axioms," those intuitions are "biases." But the word choice is doing rhetorical work that the underlying epistemology doesn't earn.

The defenders' standard reply is that there's a real asymmetry, and it does the work the rhetoric pretends. Impartiality, they say, runs *against* evolutionary pressure. Evolution generally produces in-group bias, kin preference, reciprocity gradients. The intuition that an unknown child in a distant country matters as much as your own niece is precisely the kind of intuition evolution would *not* produce. So the impartial intuition can't be debunked the way the partial intuitions can — there's no evolutionary story that explains it away, because there's no evolutionary story that explains it at all. It survived the debunking arms race by going in the wrong direction.

This is a genuinely elegant move, and it's worth taking seriously. There is something to it. But it doesn't resolve the worry as cleanly as proponents suggest.

First, the move only covers impartiality. It doesn't cover the more basic intuition that pleasure is good and suffering is bad. *That* intuition is very obviously evolutionary. Pain is bad in a phenomenal sense partly because organisms that didn't experience pain as bad got eaten. The intuition that suffering matters could be debunked using exactly the same machinery, if one wanted to. The reply will be that "pain is bad" is grounded in phenomenal acquaintance rather than inference, so it has a different epistemic status. Maybe. But that's a substantive metaethical claim that requires its own defence, not a reason the debunking argument can't reach the intuition.

Second, the "impartiality runs against evolution" move proves slightly less than it needs to. It's true that strong impartiality runs against evolutionary pressure. But the *capacity* for impartial reasoning didn't fall out of the sky. Evolution gave us general-purpose reasoning that, applied beyond its original domain, generates impartial conclusions as a byproduct. That doesn't necessarily debunk the conclusions — math and physics are also byproducts of general-purpose reasoning, and we trust them. But it does mean the impartial conclusions aren't quite as evolution-free as the move pretends. They're the product of an evolved cognitive apparatus generalised beyond its evolved purpose.

Third, and this is the most uncomfortable point: the selectivity is suspicious in the same way that motivated reasoning is generally suspicious. If you have a tool that produces verdicts in favour of your prior commitments and you don't apply it to your prior commitments themselves, you should worry that you're using the tool as a weapon rather than as a method. The fact that you can articulate a principled-sounding reason for the asymmetry doesn't dissolve the worry. People who selectively apply epistemic tools can always articulate principled-sounding reasons. Whether the reasons are principled or post hoc is hard to tell from the inside.

What would symmetrical debunking look like? It would mean running the argument against utilitarianism's own foundational intuitions and seeing what survives. Maybe the answer is "not much" — maybe all moral intuitions are evolutionary, all are debunkable, and we end up with a sceptical conclusion. Maybe the answer is "the same things survive that the utilitarians keep" — in which case the asymmetry is principled and the selectivity isn't a problem. Maybe the answer is "different things survive" — in which case the utilitarian project takes a real hit.

The honest move is to do the experiment. The current state of the literature is mostly the asymmetric move, asserted with varying degrees of confidence. That's not a fatal flaw in the utilitarian project. But it's a methodological caution worth holding onto. Debunking arguments are most reliable when they're applied symmetrically. Selective debunking is a tool, and like all tools, it works best when the person wielding it doesn't get to choose where the blade falls.
