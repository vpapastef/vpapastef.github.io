Most moral theories aspire to be public. They want to be the kind of thing you could write on a poster, hand to a stranger, defend in a city square. Kantian universalisability builds this requirement into the foundation. Rawls's publicity condition makes it explicit. Scanlon's contractualism asks what principles no one could reasonably reject — which presupposes that the principles are out in the open to be rejected.

Utilitarianism, at its limit, can't honour this aspiration. And the people who notice this most clearly are the utilitarians themselves.

The problem is the criterion/decision-procedure distinction. The utilitarian criterion of rightness is: the act that produces the best consequences. The utilitarian decision procedure — the heuristic actually used by agents in real time — is almost always something else. Calculating consequences live, for every action, is computationally impossible and motivationally destabilising. So utilitarian theory has long held that we should follow simple rules (don't lie, don't break promises, don't kill) most of the time, and only consult the criterion in unusual cases. R.M. Hare formalised this as two-level utilitarianism: an "intuitive" level for daily life, a "critical" level for unusual cases and theoretical reflection.

This is sensible as far as it goes. But it raises a question Sidgwick saw clearly and answered honestly: what about the cases where the right thing to do is to *break* a generally useful rule? The utilitarian answer is, sometimes, that you should break it — and *not* publicise the fact that you broke it, because the publicity would erode the rule's overall usefulness.

This is the doctrine of *esoteric morality*. The truth about which acts are right may diverge from the rules that are best for most people to follow, and the divergence shouldn't always be made public.

Sidgwick buried this discussion late in *The Methods of Ethics*. He understood the optics. He was a careful man writing for a Victorian audience that contained, among others, the legal apparatus that could prosecute his friend J.A. Symonds for homosexuality. He had practical reasons for not advertising the view loudly. But he committed to it. Singer and de Lazari-Radek, a century and change later, defend it explicitly.

Bernard Williams gave the canonical objection. He called it *Government House utilitarianism*: a colonial administrator in the colonies, knowing the truth about consequences, telling the natives a useful fiction. The image is damning because the structure is exactly the same — an elite that knows the real moral story, a public that's told a simplified version for its own good, justified by aggregate outcomes.

The reply Singer and de Lazari-Radek offer has two prongs.

First, they distinguish transparency from publicity. Esoteric morality requires that *some* acts not be publicly defended, but it doesn't require that the theory itself be hidden. You can write a book about esoteric morality. You can teach it in a university. What you can't do is announce, before each occasion, "I am about to break the rule for greater-good reasons."

Second, they argue that the alternative is worse. Insisting that morality always be public means accepting verdicts that produce worse outcomes — refusing to lie when the lie would save lives, refusing to break a promise when keeping it would harm everyone involved. The publicity condition, on this view, is a moral fetish that puts the form of moral practice ahead of the substance.

These are real responses. But they don't resolve the harder version of the worry, which is methodological.

The harder version: *who decides who counts as the elite?* Two-level utilitarianism asks ordinary people to follow rules and reserves the criterion-level for those "capable of better judgment." Who decides that? Presumably the people who already think they're capable of better judgment. The selection mechanism is self-flattering by construction. Given humanity's track record at this — given how many disastrous moral decisions have been made by people convinced of their own superior moral insight — the move requires a degree of caution that the theory doesn't really provide.

The most honest moment in the entire discussion is at the end of the section. Singer and de Lazari-Radek acknowledge that publishing a defence of esoteric morality is itself a paradox. By writing the book, they're partially dismantling the very condition the doctrine relies on — esoteric morality works best when most people don't know it exists. They acknowledge that the reader's resistance to the conclusion is the *correct* response. They are not asking the reader to enthusiastically adopt the view. They're asking the reader to see why a careful consequentialist can't avoid it.

This is intellectually honest in a way that is rare in philosophy. The view defeats its own promulgation, the authors notice, and the noticing doesn't make the view false. It makes the view a strange object — true (they think) but uncomfortable to be in possession of, and even more uncomfortable to argue for in public.

The comparison the authors invite is to Sidgwick's burial of the topic in the back of *The Methods of Ethics*. He hid it not because he was ashamed of it but because publicising it would have done the kind of damage the view itself predicts. Two centuries later, Singer and de Lazari-Radek surface the discussion and acknowledge the paradox openly. This is progress of a kind. But the underlying problem hasn't gone away. A moral theory whose fullest defence requires apologising for its own existence is a strange thing to hold. It might still be true. But it's a doctrine you can't quite endorse without losing some of the conditions that made it work in the first place.

Maybe that's the lesson. Some moral truths, if they are truths, are not the kind of thing that fit comfortably in a public square. The discomfort isn't an argument against them. But it is information about the shape of moral knowledge — and about the limits of what a public moral practice can ever be.
