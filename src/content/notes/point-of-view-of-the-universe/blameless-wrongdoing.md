Ordinary moral talk treats "X did the wrong thing" and "X is blameworthy for doing X" as more or less the same claim. If you did wrong, you are blameworthy. If you are not blameworthy, you cannot have done wrong. The two ride together so closely that prising them apart sounds like evasion — like the kind of move someone makes when they are trying to wriggle out of accountability.

Utilitarianism prises them apart anyway, and the prise is one of the most important and least-discussed moves in consequentialist theory.

The reason utilitarianism needs the distinction is structural. Consequences are hard to predict. Real-time calculation of expected utility for every action is impossible and motivationally destabilising. Anyone who tried to live by the criterion of rightness directly — "perform the act that maximises aggregate welfare" — would, by the criterion's own standards, produce worse outcomes than someone following sensible rules. So consistent utilitarianism cannot demand that agents apply the criterion live. It has to allow agents to follow decision procedures, and it has to be possible for those decision procedures to deliver bad outcomes despite being the best procedures available.

This generates the gap. The agent who followed the best decision procedure and got a bad outcome did the *wrong thing* substantively — the outcome was suboptimal, and a clairvoyant utilitarian could have seen in advance that it would be. But the agent is not *blameworthy* for doing the wrong thing, because they followed the procedure they were rationally required to follow. They had no access to clairvoyance. They could not have done better given what they knew. The wrong-action verdict is preserved at the level of consequence. The blame question is settled separately, at the level of procedure.

Sidgwick saw this. Hare formalised it as two-level utilitarianism. Singer and de Lazari-Radek defend it explicitly in TPOVOTU. The structure is now standard. The criterion of rightness operates at the level of outcomes; blameworthiness operates at the level of procedure-following. An agent who followed the best procedure is blameless even when the outcome was wrong. An agent who violated the best procedure is blameworthy even when the outcome was good.

That second clause is non-obvious and worth dwelling on. The *lucky rule-breaker* is, on this view, blameworthy. The person who lies to avoid a difficult conversation, and discovers later that the lie produced better consequences than the truth would have, is still blameworthy for lying — because the decision procedure they applied was worse than the one they should have applied, and they got the good outcome by luck rather than by following the procedure that would have reliably tracked it. This bites against ordinary intuition. Ordinary moral practice tends to forgive the lucky rule-breaker and focus blame on actions that produced visible harm. Utilitarianism reverses this. Procedure-violations are blameworthy regardless of how the outcome turned out. Procedure-following is blameless regardless of how the outcome turned out.

This is uncomfortable but defensible. The defenders' reply to the standard objections is that ordinary moral practice is just confused on this point, that the conflation of "did wrong" with "blameworthy" is a folk-psychological residue rather than a deep moral truth, and that utilitarianism does us a favour by prising the two apart. The reply works for many cases. The lucky rule-breaker really is doing something we should want to discourage, even when their luck holds. The conscientious rule-follower really does not deserve censure for outcomes they could not have predicted. So far, so good.

Where it gets harder is Bernard Williams's pressure. Williams's critique of utilitarianism via moral luck is partly about exactly this gap. The structure of blameless wrongdoing makes utilitarianism look like it is evading the agent-centred force of moral responsibility. "I did the wrong thing but I am not really to blame" is a position no ordinary moral practice would tolerate, and the fact that utilitarianism is forced to it by structural facts about decision-making under uncertainty suggests, to Williams, that something has gone wrong upstream. The theory has separated wrongness from responsibility in a way that ordinary moral life refuses to.

The defenders bite this bullet. They say that ordinary moral life is wrong on this point and utilitarianism is right. The agent who acted from sound principles and produced bad outcomes really should not blame themselves; they should learn what they can and move on. The reorganisation of moral practice around the criterion/procedure distinction is something we should welcome rather than resist. The agent-centred picture Williams defends is a leftover from non-consequentialist frameworks that we should have outgrown.

Whether this reply succeeds depends on how much agent-centred structure you think moral practice needs in order to function. There is a real worry that prising blame from wrongness this radically erodes the moral pressure that makes the criterion bite at all. If I can be told "yes, you did the wrong thing, but you are blameless," what work is the wrongness verdict doing in my deliberation? It cannot motivate guilt — guilt requires blameworthiness. It cannot motivate change — I already followed the best procedure I had. The wrongness verdict ends up as a kind of cosmic report card with no follow-through, which raises the question of what it adds beyond the procedural assessment.

But the deepest cost of accepting blameless wrongdoing is not the Williams worry. It is the structural one.

Once you accept the criterion/procedure distinction in order to defuse the blame problem, you have accepted a kind of architecture that has other uses. The same distinction, applied differently, generates esoteric morality. The same distinction, applied differently still, generates Government-House utilitarianism. The same distinction generates the publicity paradox.

These are not separate doctrines. They are the same architecture seen from different angles. Utilitarianism needs the criterion/procedure split in order to be liveable, and once it has the split, esoteric morality, blameless wrongdoing, and the Government-House structure all fall out as consequences. You cannot accept one of these and reject the others on principled grounds. They are made of the same machinery.

Singer and de Lazari-Radek are unusually honest about this. They accept esoteric morality, accept blameless wrongdoing, and accept that the theory has a two-level structure with an elite (those who can reliably reason at the criterion-level) and a public (who should follow simple rules). The honesty is admirable. The cost is high. What you get, at the end, is a moral theory that asks ordinary people to follow rules they do not fully understand, exempts them from blame when those rules produce bad outcomes, and reserves the right of criterion-level judgement for a class of people whose identity is not entirely specified. This is not a small concession buried in the footnotes. It is the structure of the view, made explicit.

You can accept this. Many serious philosophers do. But it is worth seeing clearly what the acceptance involves. Blameless wrongdoing is not just a clever technical move that defuses an objection. It is the visible surface of an architecture that runs through utilitarian theory at multiple levels, and once that architecture is in place, the costs cluster. The same machinery that lets the conscientious agent off the blame hook lets the elite hide moral truth from the public. The same machinery that lets us forgive bad outcomes when procedure was good lets us forgive concealment when consequences are better. The doctrines are not separable.

If you are an honest consequentialist, you should accept all of this together. If you reject any one piece, you owe an account of how you keep the others without it. Singer and de Lazari-Radek accept all of it. Most readers, on reflection, want to keep blameless wrongdoing while rejecting the architecture's other uses. That position is structurally unstable. The architecture is one piece.
