Henry Sidgwick called it the profoundest problem of ethics. He spent thirty years on it and decided, at the end, that he could not solve it.

Here is the problem, in a case you can run yourself.

You have some money you don't need this month. You could put it into a pension, where it will support you at seventy. Or you could give it to someone who needs it now more than you will need it then.

Both options answer to something that looks like a reason. The pension answers to the thought that your own life goes better or worse as a whole, and that the you of forty years from now has a claim on you today. The gift answers to the thought that suffering is bad wherever it occurs, and that its being *yours* rather than someone else's doesn't make it count for more.

Sidgwick thought both of those were genuine principles, and that neither could be derived from the other. He called the first prudence: your welfare across your whole life matters, and a pain in 2055 is not made smaller by being far off. He called the second rational benevolence: everyone's welfare matters, and yours is not weightier for being yours. Each one, he thought, survives careful reflection. Neither needs propping up by anything more basic.

The trouble is that they conflict, and they conflict constantly. Almost every time your interests run against someone else's, the two principles deliver opposite verdicts, and Sidgwick could find no third principle that ranked them. This is the *dualism of practical reason*. At the close of *The Methods of Ethics* he admitted he could not resolve it, and that admission shaped how moral philosophy was read for the next century. If Sidgwick could not bridge the gap between self-interest and impartial concern, perhaps the gap was real.

---

Derek Parfit's response, in *Reasons and Persons*, was to attack the assumption underneath the split.

Sidgwick took it for granted that the line between you and other people is deep. Future-you is *you*; future-others are not. That difference in kind is what makes prudence a separate principle in the first place. Prudence governs the special relation you have to yourself over time; benevolence governs your relation to everyone else.

Parfit denied the deep fact. On his view, being the same person over time is not some further ingredient sitting behind the ordinary connections. It just *is* those connections: memory, character, intention carried forward, the physical continuity of a brain. There is no soul underneath doing the work, no extra property of Same-Person that travels along.

None of this starts with Parfit. Hume went looking for the self in 1739 and reported that he could never catch himself without some perception already in the way. What he found was "a bundle or collection of different perceptions," with nothing standing behind them owning the collection. He then admitted, in an appendix, that he could not get his own account to come out right, and left it unresolved.

Which is worth pausing on. The two philosophers here who stated their problem most clearly, Hume on the self and Sidgwick on practical reason, both ended by conceding they were stuck. That is some evidence the difficulty sits in the material rather than in them.

Try it on yourself. How much do you share with the you of fifty years ago? Some memories, most of them rewritten. A few commitments. Very little of the body. And with the you of fifty years from now, if you get there: less than you'd like to think. Now compare either of those with someone you've lived beside for thirty years, who knows what you'll say before you say it.

Two different things are being measured there, and it matters a great deal which one prudence is leaning on.

The first is the chain. Day to day you are linked to the next you, and those links run unbroken all the way out to seventy-five. Your friend is not on that chain, and no amount of closeness puts them on it. That much really is all or nothing.

The second is how much is actually shared: memories in common, plans in common, values you'd both still defend, knowing what the other will do before they do it. That comes in degrees, and across fifty years it thins badly. With someone you have lived beside for thirty years it can be thick.

Now notice which one is doing the emotional work. When you put money aside for your seventies, you are not moved by the unbroken chain. You are moved by the thought that the person at the other end is someone you share something with. And measured that way, your friend today may have the *stronger* connection to you, not the weaker one. The chain and the sharing come apart, and prudence has been quietly claiming the authority of the first while trading on the appeal of the second.

That is enough to unsettle Sidgwick's setup. If the specialness of your future self is a matter of how much continuity there is, rather than an all-or-nothing fact about identity, then prudence loses its claim to be a separate principle resting on a separate relation.

---

But here is where the post I first drafted got it wrong, and where the book is careful.

Parfit's conclusion is that we should reject the Self-interest Theory: the view that you have most reason to do whatever will make your own life go best. What he does *not* argue is that impartial benevolence is what replaces it. The Self-interest Theory is squeezed from two sides, and impartial morality is only one of them. The other is the Present-aim Theory, on which you have most reason to do what best fulfils the aims you actually have right now.

That second candidate is the uncomfortable one. If Present-aim wins the vacated ground, you don't get impartiality. You get a view on which your future self has no special claim on you *and* neither does anybody else, except where your current desires happen to reach. The wall between you and others comes down, and nothing impartial is guaranteed to be standing behind it.

So the dualism doesn't so much heal as move. Sidgwick's two principles stop being rivals of the same rank, but the question of what practical reason actually requires is reopened rather than settled.

---

It's also worth pricing what the reductionist route costs even if impartialism does win.

If your claim on your own future is a matter of degree, tracking continuity, then the you of fifty years from now has something like the standing of a reasonably close other person. Sacrificing heavily for them is no more rationally required than sacrificing heavily for anyone else you're that close to.

Almost nobody lives this way. We save, we plan, we endure things now for a person we will only partly be. Parfit's view says that this pattern rests on a picture of identity that doesn't hold up. Maybe so. But it is a large thing to be told, and there is no comfortable middle position: either you take the reductionism and reorganise your relation to your own future, or you keep that relation and give up the dissolution.

There's a further gap I keep circling, and I'm not certain it survives scrutiny. Prudence, as Sidgwick states it, covers my welfare across time. Benevolence covers everyone's welfare. Read strictly, neither one directly addresses *your* welfare across time from *my* point of view; it seems to have to be assembled from the two. I may be reading the axiom of benevolence too narrowly, since Sidgwick's formulation about the good of any one individual counting no more than another's carries no obvious time limit. But if the gap is real, it's a different complaint from the dualism, and unifying the principles under continuity doesn't obviously fix it. Dissolving a conflict between two principles is not the same as completing either one. This connects to what the [earlier entry on foundations](/reading/point-of-view-of-the-universe/good-as-an-irreducible-property/) was circling.

---

So the verdict is mixed, and less triumphant than I first wrote it.

The deep-fact picture of identity does look shaky once you press on it, and Sidgwick's dualism does depend on that picture. That's real progress on a problem its author gave up on. But the progress buys less than it appears to. It removes self-interest's claim to privilege without installing impartiality in its place, and it asks you to hold your own future more loosely than almost anyone manages.

Sidgwick's melancholy closing thought was that practical reason might be two-headed. Parfit's answer is that the second head was never a separate one. That may be right. It leaves you needing a new account of what the remaining head is telling you to do.
