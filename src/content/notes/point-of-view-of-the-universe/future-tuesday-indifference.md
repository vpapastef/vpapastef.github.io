Parfit asks us to imagine a man who is fully indifferent to any pain he will suffer on a future Tuesday.

He cares about avoiding pain on every other day of the week. He understands that Tuesday pain will hurt just as much as Wednesday pain. He understands that the Tuesday version will be just as much *his* pain. He understands that "Tuesday" is an arbitrary calendar convention with no metaphysical depth. He simply doesn't care about Tuesday-pain. Offered a choice between mild discomfort on Wednesday and agony on Tuesday, he chooses the agony.

Parfit stipulates that this isn't based on any false belief. The man isn't confused. He doesn't think Tuesday-self is a different person. He doesn't think the pain will be milder than reported. The indifference is a brute fact about his preference structure.

The thought experiment is meant to do work against a specific picture of rationality, namely the Humean view that rationality just *is* the internal coherence of preferences. On that picture, reason has no power to evaluate ends; it only evaluates whether your means line up with whatever ends you happen to have. The Future-Tuesday-Indifferent man's preferences are perfectly coherent. There is no contradiction in his desire structure. He is not pursuing incompatible goals or harbouring inconsistent beliefs. He simply has an arbitrary discount on a specific day.

And yet almost everyone, on hearing the case, judges him irrational.

Parfit's conclusion is direct: "preferring the worse of two pains, for no reason, is irrational." Coherence isn't enough. Some preference patterns are intrinsically irrational regardless of their internal consistency. Some ends are open to rational evaluation, not just the means used to pursue them.

This is a small example doing very large work. If accepted, it establishes that there is a substantive standard of rationality that goes beyond formal consistency. And once you have that standard, several other moves open up.

The most important is the parity argument. If arbitrary *temporal* distinctions (like which day of the week pain happens on) cannot legitimately make pain matter less, then by parity of reasoning, arbitrary *personal* distinctions shouldn't either. The fact that pain is mine rather than yours is, from a sufficiently impartial standpoint, just another arbitrary feature — like the fact that pain falls on Tuesday rather than Wednesday. The two arbitrarinesses run on the same logical structure.

This is how Parfit smuggles impartiality past the Humean. He doesn't argue for it directly. He argues that you've already accepted the standard of rationality that requires it, the moment you judged the Tuesday case as irrational.

Sharon Street, in 2009, gave the best defence of the Tuesday-indifferent agent. Her move is to imagine the indifference as *tenseless* — the man remains indifferent to Tuesday pain even while experiencing it. He notices the agony, registers it as agony, and doesn't mind. Like a Buddhist who has cultivated radical detachment from sensation. On Street's reading, this person isn't really suffering in the morally relevant sense. He's having a sensation he doesn't endorse as bad. And if he doesn't endorse it as bad, the rest of us are in a weak position to insist his preferences are irrational. We're just imposing our hedonic-endorsement framework on a person who's outside it.

The Street move is sharp but it does a lot of work. To make it run, the Tuesday-indifferent agent has to be not just preference-indifferent but *experientially* indifferent — and at that point you've changed the case. The original Parfit example was a person who would, in the moment, find the pain awful, but who currently doesn't care about that future awfulness. That's the case that feels irrational. Street's revised case — a person who genuinely doesn't experience the pain as bad — feels less like a Tuesday-indifferent agent and more like an enlightened ascetic, and asceticism is a separate philosophical conversation.

So Parfit's case probably survives. And if it survives, the parity move probably survives with it. That's a substantial result for what looks at first glance like a contrived thought experiment.

But here's the part that's worth taking seriously even if you accept the result. The argument works by exploiting our intuitions about a specific kind of arbitrariness. *Tuesday* is supposed to be obviously arbitrary in a way that calls our judgment cleanly. And it is. But not every distinction between persons or moments looks that arbitrary from the inside. The distinction between *me* and *not-me* feels, phenomenologically, like something far deeper than the distinction between Tuesday and Wednesday. Parfit's move relies on convincing you that this depth-feeling is an illusion — that the self is metaphysically thinner than common sense pictures it.

This is exactly what his theory of personal identity in Part III of *Reasons and Persons* is designed to do. The Tuesday argument and the reductionist view of the self are doing coordinated work. You can't really accept the first without eventually accepting the second.

So Future Tuesday Indifference isn't just a critique of Humean subjectivism. It's the front door to the entire Parfitian programme. Walking through it commits you to a lot more than you might think.
