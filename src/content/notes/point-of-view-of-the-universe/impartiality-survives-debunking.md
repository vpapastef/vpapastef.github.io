The most powerful argument against moral realism, in the last twenty years, has been the evolutionary debunking argument. It runs like this. Our moral intuitions are produced by evolutionary processes. Those processes selected for whatever increased reproductive fitness, not for whatever happened to be morally true. So even if there were moral truths, we'd have no reason to expect our intuitions to be tracking them. The intuitions we have are the ones we'd have either way. That's not a foundation strong enough to support knowledge.

Sharon Street put the argument in its most formidable form. Richard Joyce ran a parallel version targeting moral judgement specifically. The conclusion, in both cases, is that moral realism faces an epistemic problem so severe that the realist's preferred response — there are moral truths, and we can know them — looks like a coincidence that requires explaining away rather than a confident position.

This is a strong argument. It bites at most ordinary moral intuitions. Kin preference? Plausibly evolved from inclusive-fitness pressures, doesn't track moral truth, debunked. In-group loyalty? Coalition-selection, debunked. Reciprocity, fairness, gratitude, indignation, the disposition to punish defectors and reward cooperators? All of these have plausible evolutionary explanations that make no reference to their being morally correct. The realist can't simply say "but they feel like moral truths" — that's what's being debunked.

But there's one intuition the argument can't reach.

The intuition that an unknown child in a distant country, whose suffering or flourishing has no effect on my genes or my reciprocal partners, *matters as much* as my own niece. This is the intuition of impartial benevolence. And it is precisely the kind of intuition evolution would never have produced.

Evolution is, fundamentally, a partiality-generating machine. It produces creatures that prefer their own offspring, their own kin, their own tribe, their own future selves. It produces gradients of concern that fall off with genetic distance, with relational closeness, with reciprocal potential. Every disposition that helped ancestors propagate their genes was, by definition, a partial disposition. A creature that genuinely treated every conspecific as equivalent in moral weight would have been outcompeted by a creature that prioritised its own kin. The math doesn't work any other way.

So if you find yourself, on reflection, judging that your niece's suffering and a stranger's suffering matter equally — that's not a verdict evolution gave you. It's a verdict your evolved cognitive apparatus, applied beyond its evolved purpose, has *generated* against the grain of what evolution would have selected for. The intuition runs against the gradient. It is, on the available evidence, *the only kind* of moral intuition the standard debunking argument can't reach, because the standard debunking move is "this was evolved, therefore unreliable" — and impartiality wasn't evolved.

This is one of the most elegant moves in *The Point of View of the Universe*. Singer and de Lazari-Radek take the debunker's tool and turn it carefully: yes, run the argument against all the partial intuitions you like; the impartial intuition was never produced by the process you're invoking, so it survives. The very feature that makes impartiality demanding and counterintuitive — that it goes against everything we instinctively feel — is also what gives it a unique epistemic status.

The move has real force. But it's worth being careful about exactly what it does and doesn't establish.

What it establishes is that the standard form of the evolutionary debunking argument doesn't reach impartiality. The argument needs the conclusion-to-be-debunked to be one evolution would have selected for, and impartiality isn't. So far, so good.

What it doesn't establish is that impartiality is therefore *true*, or even particularly likely to be true. The fact that an intuition runs against evolutionary pressure isn't, by itself, evidence that the intuition tracks moral reality. Some intuitions running against evolution might just be cognitive accidents — byproducts of general-purpose reasoning applied where it doesn't naturally apply. The Tuesday-indifferent man's preferences run against evolution too (evolution selected for *symmetric* time-preference structures, not arbitrary discounts on calendar days), but we don't think that makes them rationally compelling.

The way to make the move stronger is to combine it with something else. If impartiality is what falls out of *general-purpose reasoning applied carefully* — the same kind of reasoning that gives us math and logic — then its evolutionary origin-resistance plus its derivation from reflection is jointly suggestive. We trust math because it's the output of a faculty that goes well beyond its evolved purpose and seems to reliably track structural truths. The case for impartiality is that it's the output of the same faculty operating in the moral domain, with the same character of pushing past evolutionary parochialism in the direction of formal structure.

This is the move Parfit makes more explicitly in *On What Matters*. The realist position isn't "intuitions are reliable because they feel true." The position is "carefully reflective intuitions, especially ones that survive consideration of debunking arguments, are the kind of thing it would be unreasonable to dismiss." The convergence of careful reflectors on certain conclusions — Sidgwick, Mill, Kant, contemporary contractualists — is itself weak evidence that the conclusions are tracking something real, even if no single intuition can be argued for from below.

So the elegant move in *Point of View of the Universe* does real work but slightly less than its proponents sometimes claim. It blocks the standard debunking move against impartiality. It doesn't, on its own, establish that impartial benevolence is the correct moral principle. What it does is shift the burden of proof: the debunker, faced with impartiality, has to come up with a *different* debunking story (not the evolutionary one), and the candidate alternatives are weaker.

This is more progress than it sounds like. Most realist positions in metaethics aren't trying to *prove* their conclusions from neutral starting points. They're trying to defend reflectively endorsed conclusions against the strongest available objections. If the strongest available objection is evolutionary debunking, and evolutionary debunking can't touch impartiality, then the realist has a defensible position on at least one core moral commitment.

That's not everything. But it's a foothold, and footholds in this part of philosophy are rare.
