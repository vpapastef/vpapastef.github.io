The desire-satisfaction theory of wellbeing keeps trying to fix itself. Each fix creates a new pathology. The Jane Austen case shows why.

Start with Parfit's addiction objection. Summative desire theory says your wellbeing equals the satisfaction of your desires, summed up. Now imagine an addiction that gives no pleasure and no pain — just a craving that can be satisfied. Each time you take the drug, your desire-satisfaction ledger ticks up. You're getting "better off." This is absurd.

The patch is elegant. Treat new desires as debts. Creating a desire is a debit; satisfying it just brings you back to zero, not above it. So inducing the addiction puts you in the red, and satisfying the craving pays off the debt. Case defused.

Now imagine someone introduces you to Jane Austen. You read all six novels, love them deeply, finish them, and wish there were more. On the debt account: the introduction created a desire (debit), six novels paid down most of it (credit), but the residual unfulfilled desire for *more Austen* sits there as a permanent debit on your books.

Net wellbeing impact of discovering Austen: negative.

This isn't a corner case. Every enriching experience that leaves you wanting more — music, friendships, places, ideas, love — generates a residual desire that won't be perfectly satisfied. The debt account commits you to saying that the ideal life is one of maximal ignorance and minimal engagement. The person who has never loved is better off than the person who has loved deeply and wants more. That isn't just counterintuitive; it inverts most of what people actually mean when they describe a life going well.

The pattern is structural. Brandt's instability worries get patched by *global* desire theories, which then stumble on the dancing case (someone whose deepest desire is to dance, who reasonably reorganizes their life around it). The addiction case gets patched by the debt framework, which then stumbles on Austen. Each fix rescues the theory from one counterexample by inviting another. The trees keep being saved; the forest keeps burning.

The deeper diagnosis is that desire-satisfaction is the wrong thing to track. Wellbeing isn't about the formal status of your preference ledger. It's something experiential or evaluative that desire-talk imperfectly proxies. As long as the theory tries to reduce wellbeing to ledger states, it will keep generating verdicts at odds with the experience it was supposed to measure.

There is one more thing worth noticing, and it's awkward for the standard utilitarian tradition.

The Austen-debt verdict has exactly the same logical structure as Benatar's asymmetry argument. Benatar says: don't create the being, because you'll create unfulfillable desires (debits) without thereby gaining anything — the unborn aren't being deprived of pleasures they were never going to get. The Austen-debt theorist says: don't create the desire, because you'll generate unfulfillable wants without offsetting goods.

These are the same move applied at different levels. Singer and de Lazari-Radek reject the first when it's applied to existence (they push back against Benatar) and accept structurally identical reasoning when it suits them in the wellbeing chapter (using the debt framing to defuse the addiction case).

The selectivity is hard to defend. If you accept the debt patch, something like antinatalism follows for free at the population level: bringing into existence is precisely the act of creating an entity whose desires will inevitably outrun their satisfactions. If you reject the debt patch, the addiction case bites again.

There is no available middle path that gets the addiction case right, the Austen case right, and lets Singer keep the procreation conclusions he wants. You have to give up one of them.

That's not a small concession buried in a footnote. It's a load-bearing inconsistency near the foundations of the view. And it's the kind of thing that becomes visible only when you read the wellbeing chapter and the population chapter side by side and notice the same structural move being used in opposite directions.

Most of utilitarian theory's interesting failures are like this — not big counterexamples that show up in introductory papers, but quiet structural tensions that emerge when you put two chapters of the same book next to each other.
