Modern welfare economics rests, in large part, on a question. The question is some version of: "Taking everything together, how satisfied are you with your life as a whole these days, on a scale from 0 to 10?"

Variants of this question power the World Values Survey, the Gallup World Poll, the OECD Better Life Index, and most national wellbeing initiatives that have emerged in the last twenty years. The premise is that life satisfaction is a good enough proxy for happiness to base policy on. The Easterlin paradox, the "happiness economics" movement, Layard's work — all of it rests on the assumption that what people *report* when asked this question tracks how their lives are actually going.

The assumption doesn't hold. And the gap between what life satisfaction surveys measure and what wellbeing actually is turns out to be both empirically large and methodologically embarrassing.

Start with the simplest finding. When researchers measure life satisfaction and emotional states in the same individuals using different instruments, the two come apart in ways that should be shocking. Twenty-nine percent of people who describe themselves as "completely satisfied" with their lives also report significant levels of anxiety and distress symptoms. Six percent of the "completely satisfied" report being usually unhappy or depressed. These aren't edge cases. These are a substantial fraction of the most-satisfied group reporting emotional lives that don't match their satisfaction score.

In ordinary conversation, "completely satisfied with my life" and "usually unhappy or depressed" sound like contradictions. In the survey data they cohabit comfortably in the same person.

What's going on is that life satisfaction is not a measurement of felt experience. It's an evaluative judgment — a cognitive act of comparing your life to some standard, often a standard borrowed from social context, expectations, or what feels like the appropriate thing to say. The judgment can run almost independently of how you actually feel from day to day.

The most dramatic demonstration of this came from Angus Deaton's analysis of Gallup data around the 2008–9 financial crisis. Reported life satisfaction in the United States actually *rose* in early 2009. This was not because people had recovered from the crisis. It was because Gallup had been preceding the satisfaction question with questions about the upcoming election, and when the election-related questions were dropped from the survey instrument, satisfaction scores jumped. The political questions had been depressing the reported figures.

The implication is severe. A "measurement" that swings significantly based on what question you happen to ask before it is not measuring anything stable about the underlying state of the person. It's measuring a momentary act of summary construction, sensitive to whatever happens to be cognitively available at the moment of asking.

This isn't an isolated finding. Life satisfaction reports are responsive to all kinds of trivial contextual factors: finding a small coin before the survey, receiving a piece of candy, whether the weather is sunny, whether the question is asked in a clean room or a cluttered one. The construct is so context-sensitive that it functions less like a thermometer and more like a Rorschach test that happens to produce a number.

If this is what life satisfaction surveys are measuring, then the entire infrastructure of happiness economics is in trouble. The Easterlin paradox — the finding that average life satisfaction in rich countries doesn't rise much with GDP — might be true, false, or unmeasurable, and the existing data can't reliably tell us which. National wellbeing initiatives that rank countries on satisfaction scores are largely ranking countries on cultural reporting norms and survey-design idiosyncrasies. The policy lessons drawn from this data are correspondingly fragile.

What's the alternative? The natural move is to measure something closer to actual experience. Experience sampling — pinging people at random moments and asking how they feel right now — gets closer to the experiencing self. Mood inventories, anxiety scales, depression scales all aim at felt experience rather than evaluative judgment. These measurements are more expensive and harder to administer, which is why economists have been reluctant to use them, but they appear to track something more stable about how people's lives are actually going.

The deeper problem is that the choice of measurement isn't methodologically neutral. It's a substantive philosophical commitment. If you measure life satisfaction, you're implicitly endorsing the view that wellbeing is constituted by reflective evaluation. If you measure emotional states, you're implicitly endorsing the view that wellbeing is constituted by felt experience. These are different theories of wellbeing, and they deliver different policy verdicts. Pretending the choice of instrument is a technical matter — about what data is easiest to collect — hides the philosophical work that the instrument is doing.

The honest position is that no single measure captures wellbeing. People have evaluative judgments about their lives, and those judgments matter for some purposes — for example, in deciding whether to change careers or end a relationship. People also have moment-to-moment hedonic experiences, and those matter for other purposes — for example, in evaluating whether a policy is making the population's daily lives go better or worse. The two come apart in many cases, and the policy question is which one we should care about *for the purpose at hand*.

The infrastructure of happiness economics has mostly answered this question by accident, picking the measure that was easiest to collect rather than the measure that best fit the policy question. The accidental answer has been good enough to support an entire field of literature. It is not good enough to support actual policy.

The 29% number — completely satisfied people who are nonetheless anxious — is the cleanest indictment of the field's central instrument that anyone has produced. The fact that it hasn't shaken the field more is itself a piece of data about how stable methodological habits can be in the face of disconfirming evidence.
