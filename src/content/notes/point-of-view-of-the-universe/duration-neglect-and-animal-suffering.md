Kahneman's most disorienting finding is one of his quietest. When we remember an experience — a meal, a holiday, a medical procedure, a stretch of pain — we don't remember the whole of it. We remember a weighted average of its peak intensity and how it ended. The duration of the experience barely figures in our retrospective evaluation.

This is the *peak-end rule*, and the corresponding phenomenon is *duration neglect*. A twenty-minute colonoscopy and a thirty-minute colonoscopy, both equally painful at peak, both ending the same way, are remembered as roughly equally bad. The extra ten minutes of pain in the second case adds almost nothing to the remembered experience. As far as the remembering self is concerned, that suffering effectively didn't happen.

This is already strange. But the strangeness compounds when you start using it to compare welfare across creatures.

Kahneman's framework distinguishes the *experiencing self* from the *remembering self*. The experiencing self is whatever is having the experience moment to moment. The remembering self is whoever is later reconstructing it for evaluation, decision, and report. These two selves often disagree, and when they do, the remembering self almost always wins — it's the one making the decisions, doing the reporting, voting in the policy survey. The experiencing self is silenced by structure, not by content.

For humans, this matters morally. It matters even more for the moral weight we assign to non-human animals.

Most animals plausibly have something like an experiencing self. They have neural correlates of pain, they exhibit pain-avoidance behaviour, they can be conditioned and traumatised. The case for moment-to-moment animal suffering is overwhelming. What's much less clear is whether most animals have anything like a robust remembering self in the human sense. A chicken in a battery cage feels each second of distress. Whether the chicken, at any later moment, reconstructs and evaluates the duration of that distress in the way a human does — that's a different and harder question, and the answer for most species is plausibly "no, or barely."

Now combine the two facts.

Fact one: duration of suffering matters morally. If twenty minutes of equivalent pain matters more than ten minutes of equivalent pain — which any halfway-reasonable moral theory grants — then total suffering scales with time spent suffering.

Fact two: duration of suffering is systematically under-weighted by remembering selves. Even in humans, who have the most developed remembering self, the bias is severe.

Combine these and you get an uncomfortable conclusion. The remembering self, *as a moral judge*, will reliably under-count suffering whose moral weight depends on duration. And the creatures most exposed to this under-counting are the ones whose suffering is mostly experiential and barely remembered at all — the animals.

Two factory-farm chickens. One spends three weeks in moderate distress. The other spends six weeks in the same distress and dies. From the chickens' point of view — from each moment-to-moment experiencing self — the second life contains twice the suffering. From any retrospective reconstruction, including any human evaluation of how bad these lives were, the difference between three weeks and six weeks barely shows up. The peak is the same. The end is the same. Duration neglect erases the gap.

If you take moment-to-moment experience seriously as the bearer of moral weight, then most of human moral perception is systematically wrong about how much animal suffering exists in the world. Not slightly wrong. *Structurally* wrong, by a factor that scales with duration. The aggregate suffering of factory-farmed animals isn't merely large — it's larger than our cognitive equipment is built to register, because the equipment was built to summarise experiences via peaks and endings, and most factory-farm experience is duration without peak.

This is a result that should change the moral conversation about animals more than it does. The standard arguments for veganism appeal to the awfulness of *individual* moments — videos of slaughterhouse cruelty, descriptions of the worst moments in a battery-cage chicken's life. These are powerful but they appeal precisely to the part of our equipment that already works well, the peak-detector. They don't address the structurally invisible part: the millions of low-grade hours that get compressed to nothing by retrospective compression.

If duration matters, the case against industrial animal agriculture isn't primarily about the cruelty of the worst moments. It's about the weight of moments that aren't dramatic enough to peak — the long unbroken stretches of confined boredom and low-grade distress that constitute most of a factory-farmed animal's existence. These hours don't get a starring role in the documentary because there's nothing to film. They are precisely the kind of suffering our minds are built to overlook.

There is a sharper version of this that's worth stating clearly. If you accept that what makes pain bad is the felt experience of the pain rather than the later evaluation of it, then the moral situation of factory farming may be much worse than even committed animal-welfare advocates believe. Not because the worst moments are worse than reported — but because the moments that don't *register* in the human remembering apparatus still happened, still mattered to the creature having them, and aggregate to a quantity of suffering the human moral imagination is structurally unequipped to hold.

The remembering self is a useful adaptation for a creature that needs to choose which restaurant to go back to. It is a terrible adaptation for a creature that needs to do impartial moral arithmetic about beings whose experience consists almost entirely of un-peakable hours.
