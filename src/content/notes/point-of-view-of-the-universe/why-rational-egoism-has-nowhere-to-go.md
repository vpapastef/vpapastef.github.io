Rational egoism — the view that I have decisive reason to pursue my own welfare and no comparable reason to pursue anyone else's — has historically been treated, in the analytic tradition, as the most resilient alternative to impartial morality. Sidgwick called the tension between egoism and impartial benevolence the "profoundest problem of ethics" and went to his grave thinking he had not solved it. Williams revived a sophisticated version of the position a century later. Gauthier built a careful contractarian programme on the assumption that egoism survives examination.

The contemporary verdict is harder on egoism than that history suggests. Singer and de Lazari-Radek's treatment in *The Point of View of the Universe* essentially closes the question — not by a single knock-down argument, but by closing six independent escape routes at once. None of the six is decisive on its own. Together, they leave egoism with nowhere defensible to stand.

The first route is *evolutionary debunking*. Egoism, kin-preference, in-group loyalty — all are precisely what evolution would produce regardless of whether they happen to be true. The intuition supporting egoism looks structurally identical to the intuition we would have if egoism were false. Without an independent reason to trust the intuition, the appeal to "but it just feels obvious that my own welfare matters more to me" is exactly the move the debunking argument was designed to neutralise.

The egoist's standard counter is that *every* moral intuition is evolved, so debunking proves too much. But this counter misfires for the specific case of impartial benevolence, which runs *against* what evolution would have selected for. Evolution does not produce impartial agents. So the playing field is not level: egoism's intuitions look like evolved noise; impartiality's intuitions look like reasoning operating against the evolutionary grain. The debunking argument does not impose symmetric costs; it imposes asymmetric ones, with egoism paying more.

The second route is *personal identity*. Egoism requires a robust view of the self: future-me has to be categorically different in kind from any other person, otherwise the special claim future-me has on present-me cannot be justified. Parfit's reductionist argument that personal identity is "not what matters" — that what we call identity is a bundle of overlapping psychological continuities admitting of degrees — dissolves the categorical distinction. Once the distinction is degree-not-kind, the egoist's "me versus not-me" boundary is no longer rationally privileged. It is just a particularly high-continuity case of "person A versus person B." Prudence becomes a special case of benevolence, and the dualism collapses into a single unified principle of impartial concern across persons-and-times.

The third route is *the parity argument from Future Tuesday Indifference*. Parfit's thought experiment forces a concession that the egoist would rather not make. Almost everyone judges the Future-Tuesday-Indifferent man irrational, even though his preferences are internally coherent. Coherence is not enough; some preference patterns are intrinsically irrational because they discount on arbitrary grounds. But "Tuesday versus Wednesday" and "me versus not-me" are structurally identical kinds of arbitrariness. If the egoist accepts the Tuesday verdict, they owe an account of why person-discounting is rationally robust where day-discounting is not. The accounts available all collapse into "I just care more about me," which is a description of motivation, not a justification of reason.

The fourth route is the sharpest of the structural attacks, and the one most people miss. The egoist cannot be a *present-moment* hedonist — they have to care about their own future welfare, otherwise they are not even an egoist. So egoism is already committed to *intra-*personal impartiality: every moment of my existence counts equally, simply because all of those moments are mine. The Tersman move, picked up and amplified by Kahane, is to point out that intra-personal impartiality is structurally identical to *inter-*personal impartiality. The reasoning that gets you "your own future welfare matters as much as your present welfare" is the same reasoning that gets you "others' welfare matters as much as your own." The egoist accepts the first and rejects the second without a principled basis for the asymmetry. The Self-Interest Principle smuggles in the very impartiality it refuses to extend.

The fifth route is *universalizability*. Hare's argument that the logic of moral terms commits the speaker to universalising was attacked by Mackie at its extreme version but does real work at the more modest version. The egoist who says "I ought to maximise my own welfare" cannot consistently refuse to extend the principle to "anyone ought to maximise their own welfare." At which point the position is no longer egoism but a principle about welfare-maximisation in general, which has impartial implications the egoist will resist. The escape route here is to refuse the moral vocabulary entirely and rephrase the position in non-prescriptive language. But then it is not a *normative* position, it is a description of motivation, and it does not compete with normative views like impartial benevolence on the same terms.

The sixth route is the one Sidgwick himself reluctantly opened. Even on the egoist's preferred epistemological method — self-evidence to rational reflection — egoism does not win cleanly. Sidgwick took the axiom of prudence and the axiom of rational benevolence to be *equally* self-evident. The best result the egoist can claim from self-evidence-style argumentation is a *tie* with benevolence, which leaves us with the dualism. And the dualism, Sidgwick acknowledged, he could not resolve. A standoff is not a victory. And once routes two and three are also in play — Parfit's reductionism and the parity argument — the standoff breaks in favour of whoever has a way out. The egoist has no such way out.

Notice what the converging structure achieves. Each of these objections is, on its own, contestable. The egoist can wriggle out of debunking by claiming the argument proves too much. They can resist personal-identity reductionism. They can deny the Tuesday parity. They can refuse universalizability. But the wriggling has to succeed *across the board*. Wriggling out of one objection does not help if four others are still landing. Egoism survives only if it survives all six attacks at once. And the moves the egoist makes to escape one attack often expose them to the others. (Reject reductionism about identity, fine — but then you have to explain why "Tuesday versus Wednesday" arbitrariness is rationally suspect while "me versus not-me" arbitrariness is not, and you have just lost your best resource for blocking the parity argument.)

What is left for the egoist after all this? Essentially what Gauthier tried to do in *Morals by Agreement* — abandon egoism as a foundational position and try to reconstruct it as a derivation from rational self-interest *plus* social-contract reasoning. This is already a substantial retreat. The position that comes out the other side is no longer egoism as Sidgwick understood it — it includes substantial cooperative commitments and a kind of constrained impartialism — and the derivation depends on contested empirical assumptions about what cooperation rational agents would converge on under idealised bargaining conditions. The view is still defensible in some form. But it is no longer the simple, intuitive "my welfare matters and others' does not" position that egoism was supposed to be.

What is striking is how thoroughly this case rests on internal moves in the utilitarian tradition itself. None of the six attacks requires importing Kantian, contractualist, or virtue-theoretic premises. Evolutionary debunking, personal identity, Tuesday parity, Tersman, modest universalizability, and Sidgwick's tie are all moves that utilitarians make, against a view that utilitarians once took seriously as their main competitor. The position that contemporary utilitarianism most decisively refutes is the one it used to treat as its hardest rival.

Egoism, after TPOVOTU, is not a live view in serious moral philosophy. It survives in introductory courses and in undergraduate debate. The reason is not that the obvious objections are powerful. It is that the non-obvious objections, taken together, leave no defensible standing ground.
