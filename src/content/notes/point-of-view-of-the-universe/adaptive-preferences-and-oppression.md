The strongest argument for hedonism over preference-satisfaction theories of wellbeing isn't a thought experiment. It's an empirical observation about what happens to people in unjust circumstances.

The observation runs like this. If you ask members of historically oppressed or subordinated groups how satisfied they are with their lives, the answers are often surprisingly high. Women in deeply patriarchal societies frequently report life satisfaction levels comparable to women in egalitarian ones. People in caste-stratified societies often report satisfaction with positions that, viewed externally, look like sites of significant suffering. In some studies, residents of the most unequal regions of a country report higher satisfaction than residents of more equal regions.

Amartya Sen labelled this phenomenon *adaptive preferences*. The mechanism is recognisable enough: people adjust their expectations to fit the conditions of their lives. The teenager who has never travelled doesn't grieve the trip she never took. The factory worker who has never imagined a different career doesn't experience his current job as a constraint on alternatives. The wife in a strictly hierarchical household doesn't desire autonomy she has never been positioned to want. The preferences contract to fit the available space.

For a preference-satisfaction theory of wellbeing, this is catastrophic. The theory says you are well-off insofar as your preferences are satisfied. If your preferences have been quietly truncated by your social position, then the theory will judge that you're doing fine — satisfied preferences, after all, are what its measure tracks. The theory will not flag oppression as a wellbeing problem. It will, in some cases, *vindicate* the oppression by registering high satisfaction scores in the oppressed group.

This is not a small problem. It means the theory cannot distinguish between a person whose desires are fulfilled because their life is genuinely going well and a person whose desires are fulfilled because their desires have been quietly amputated by their conditions. Both come out the same on the ledger. From the inside of the theory, there is nothing to choose between them.

Hedonism handles this much better. Hedonism doesn't ask what you wanted and whether you got it. It asks what you actually feel. And what the empirical literature suggests is that even when oppressed people *report* high life satisfaction, their actual emotional lives don't match. They show elevated rates of anxiety, depression, somatic complaints, sleep disturbance, and the other measurable correlates of hedonic distress. The evaluative report has adapted; the felt experience often hasn't.

The hedonist can therefore say something the preference theorist can't. *Yes, you report being satisfied. No, your life is not going well. The satisfaction is itself a symptom of the conditions that are damaging you, not evidence that you are unscathed by them.* This is an unusual move for a welfare theory to make — it explicitly overrules the person's self-report in favour of an external measure of their wellbeing — but it's the right verdict in these cases, and it's the verdict any theory worth taking seriously needs to be able to deliver.

There is a residual worry worth taking seriously. What if the adaptation is *complete*? Imagine someone whose hedonic state has, somehow, fully adjusted to oppressive conditions — not just their reported satisfaction but their actual moment-to-moment experience. They genuinely feel contentment. They experience no anxiety, no distress, no somatic complaints. From the inside, their life feels uniformly good. From the outside, they're a subordinated person in conditions we would normally call oppressive.

For the strict hedonist, this is a hard case. The theory has to say: their life *is* going well, hedonically, and that's what wellbeing consists in. The case is uncomfortable but the theory has to bite the bullet — otherwise it's not really hedonism, it's hedonism-plus-some-other-thing.

Two responses are available.

The first is the *empirical bet*. Complete hedonic adaptation to oppression, the hedonist can argue, is essentially a philosopher's fiction. Real human psychology doesn't work this way. Oppression leaves hedonic traces — in stress hormones, in sleep, in chronic anxiety, in somatic symptoms — even when it has erased every conscious complaint. The complete-adaptation scenario is not something that actually happens at scale, and a theory shouldn't be judged primarily by its verdicts on cases that don't occur.

The second is the *acknowledgment*. If, against the empirical bet, complete hedonic adaptation is genuinely possible, then hedonism really does deliver an uncomfortable verdict on such cases. And at that point, the right move is probably to leave hedonism behind for something like Sen's capability approach. On the capability view, wellbeing isn't constituted only by how you feel; it's also constituted by what you're *able* to do and be. The capability approach handles even complete-adaptation cases cleanly: the person whose hedonic state has fully adapted to subordination is still being deprived of capabilities they would have valued if they'd been positioned to have them, and that deprivation is a wellbeing harm regardless of how the person feels about it.

So the practical map is: hedonism for almost all real cases, including most of what the literature on adaptive preferences actually documents, and capability theory as the fallback for the philosopher's-fiction case where adaptation is total.

Either way, preference-satisfaction theory loses. The empirical literature on adaptive preferences makes it almost impossible to maintain that the satisfaction of someone's actual preferences is a reliable measure of how their life is going. Preferences are not given. They are produced. The conditions that produce them include, prominently, conditions of injustice and constraint. Any theory that measures wellbeing by counting satisfied preferences is, in effect, counting how well the conditions of injustice have worked on the person.

That's not a measure of wellbeing. That's a measure of how successfully someone has been disciplined to want less.
